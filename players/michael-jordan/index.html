Michael Jordan: Check the Scorer and the Ball Hawk

Jordan is a scoring pick with a steals question attached. In the 82-0 game, the interesting choice is what you keep in both columns—and what the other card offers back.

Both boxes count titles, so they share a unit. They give a different view of the scoring-and-takeaways profile than the career averages do. The boxes are not additional PPG and SPG to attach to a draft card.

A steals lead measures credited takeaways, not every part of defense. Read the steals explanation when you want to understand the smaller decimal before weighing the guard choices.

💬Put the two-column question to the cards

Jordan leads in points. What happens to steals?

If he also leads in SPG, you have two visible advantages to carry into the full comparison. If the alternative leads in SPG, the extra scoring has a stated cost. Read RPG, APG and BPG next; the two-column opening is a starting point, not a substitute for the rest of the row.

The Jordan card looks lower than I expected. Is it still useful?

Compare it with the guard you can actually choose, using the same destination constraints. A less spectacular period can still be the better fit among those options. Comparing it only with a remembered Bulls peak gives you no answer about the current alternative.

A better reason than “it's Jordan”

“Keep the scoring and steals leads; accept the alternative's rebound advantage.”

That note is useful only if those are the differences printed on the two cards. It names the gain and the loss, letting you review the decision later. You can write an equally clear note when you reject Jordan for a different combination of stats.

Use the five-field comparison for the complete exchange. In the 82-0 game, a good Jordan choice should explain both the scoring attraction and the ball-hawk question.

Give both guards a fair description: the scoring gain, the steals change and the destination each can use.

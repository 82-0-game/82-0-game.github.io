Steals Per Game: Follow the Credit

A touch, a deflection and a possession-winning steal are different events. Use this 82-0 game casebook to read SPG without losing the credit rules or the decimals.

🖐️Who gets the steal?

A steal is about a defensive action that gains possession, not just a hand touching the ball. Open these three situations to follow the credit. They explain basketball events; they are not claims about a hidden possession simulation in 82-0.

Case 1 · A defender intercepts a pass

The defender reads the pass and takes possession. The interception supplies the steal credit. A pass can look risky without producing a steal if the offensive team keeps the ball.

Case 2 · One defender deflects, a teammate recovers

A controlled deflection causes the opponent to lose possession, and a teammate secures the ball. The defender responsible for the deflection can receive the steal. Credit does not automatically follow the person who finishes holding it.

Case 3 · The attacker retains the deflected ball

The defender disrupts the pass, but the attacking team recovers and continues. The touch alone does not supply a possession-winning steal. Disruption and credited takeaway are different observations.

The 82-0 game card gives you an average, not the sequence needed to distinguish these patterns. It would be a mistake to attach a consistency bonus or penalty to either pattern as though the game had displayed it.

🔬Do not turn half a steal into one

A displayed comparison of 1.2 versus 1.7 has a gap of 0.5 SPG. Round each to the nearest whole number and you get 1 versus 2: the apparent gap doubles to 1. Truncate both instead and you get 1 versus 1: the gap disappears.

Neither shortcut preserves the printed comparison. Copy the decimals first, then subtract within the category. The rounding workbench also explains why adding printed card numbers may differ slightly from a team total formed before display rounding.

What SPG does not settle

Takeaways are valuable, but SPG does not describe every defensive possession. A defender who forces a difficult shot, denies a pass or contains a drive can help without recording a steal. Conversely, this one average does not reveal how often a player gambled and left a teammate exposed.

SPG uses appearances, rather than minutes or possessions, as its denominator. Equal SPG does not establish equal takeaway rates for every minute played or every possession defended. Those comparisons need information beyond the five displayed averages.

Read the contribution the card actually supplies. When two legal entries exchange SPG for points or rebounds, use the category comparison rather than calling one the better overall defender.
